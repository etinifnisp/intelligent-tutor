Question: Spherometer cannot measure which of the following quantities? Options: (a) Radius of curvature of convex lens (b) Radius of curvature of concave lens (c) thickness of thin plates. (d) specific rotation of liquids Answer: (d)
Concept Tested
💡 A spherometer is a precision instrument designed to measure small thicknesses and the radius of curvature of spherical surfaces (both convex and concave).
Step-by-Step Solution
A spherometer consists of three outer legs forming an equilateral triangle and a central screw that can move vertically. 1. **Radius of curvature of convex and concave surfaces (Options a and b):** The formula to find the radius of curvature $R$ of a spherical surface is given by: $$R = \frac{l^2}{6h} + \frac{h}{2}$$ where $l$ is the average distance between the legs of the spherometer and $h$ is the height (sagitta) measured by the central screw. Since $h$ can be measured for both convex (upward bulge) and concave (downward depression) surfaces, a spherometer can measure the radius of curvature of both convex and concave lenses. 2. **Thickness of thin plates (Option c):** The thickness $t$ of a thin plate is measured by taking the difference between the zero-reading on a flat surface and the reading when the plate is placed under the central screw, i.e., $t = h$. 3. **Specific rotation of liquids (Option d):** Specific rotation is an optical property of chiral substances in liquid form, defining the angle of rotation of plane-polarized light. This is measured using a **polarimeter**, not a mechanical distance-measuring instrument like a spherometer. Hence, a spherometer cannot measure the specific rotation of liquids.
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (d)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ A common misconception is that a spherometer can only measure convex surfaces because of the central screw, but it is equally capable of measuring concave surfaces by rotating the screw downwards below the plane of the legs. Another error is confusing optical measurements (like polarization and rotation) with mechanical dimensions.

Question: Find Moment of Inertia of Massless square frame of side length 2 m with mass of 1 Kg on Each Vertex about axis passing through a side Options: (a) 8 kgm2 (b) 2 kgm2 (c) 4 kgm2 (d) 3 kgm2 Answer: (a)
Concept Tested
💡 The moment of inertia $I$ of a system of discrete point masses is given by the sum of the products of each mass and the square of its perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation: $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$.
Step-by-Step Solution
Consider a square frame of side length $a = 2\text{ m}$ with four point masses of $m = 1\text{ kg}$ placed at each of its four vertices, say $A, B, C,$ and $D$. Let the axis of rotation pass through one of the sides of the square, for instance, the side $AB$. 1. The perpendicular distance of the masses at vertices $A$ and $B$ from the axis of rotation (line $AB$) is zero, since they lie directly on the axis: $$r_A = 0\text{ m}$$ $$r_B = 0\text{ m}$$ 2. The perpendicular distance of the masses at vertices $C$ and $D$ from the axis of rotation is equal to the side length of the square: $$r_C = a = 2\text{ m}$$ $$r_D = a = 2\text{ m}$$ 3. The total moment of inertia $I$ of the system is the sum of the moments of inertia of the individual point masses: $$I = m_A r_A^2 + m_B r_B^2 + m_C r_C^2 + m_D r_D^2$$ Substituting the given values: $$I = 1 \cdot (0)^2 + 1 \cdot (0)^2 + 1 \cdot (2)^2 + 1 \cdot (2)^2$$ $$I = 0 + 0 + 4 + 4 = 8\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$$ Therefore, the moment of inertia of the system is $8\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$.
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: (a)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ A common mistake is using the diagonal length ($a\sqrt{2}$) as the distance of the furthest masses from the axis, rather than their perpendicular distance to the side. Another error is assuming that all four masses contribute equally, forgetting that the two masses lying directly on the axis of rotation have a perpendicular distance of zero.

Question: A beaker is filled with two liquids each of height 6 cm if refractive indices of two liquids are μ1 = 8/3 and μ2 = 5/3 then find the apparent depth of a coin kept at the bottom of the container Options: (a) 4.85 cm (b) 5.85 cm (c) 6.85 cm (d) 7.85 cm Answer: (b)
Concept Tested
💡 The apparent depth of an object kept at the bottom of a container filled with multiple immiscible liquid layers is the sum of the apparent depths of the individual layers, given by $d' = \sum \frac{h_i}{\mu_i}$, where $h_i$ and $\mu_i$ are the thickness and refractive index of the $i$-th layer respectively.
Step-by-Step Solution
We are given: - Thickness of the first liquid layer, $h_1 = 6\text{ cm}$ - Refractive index of the first liquid layer, $\mu_1 = \frac{8}{3}$ - Thickness of the second liquid layer, $h_2 = 6\text{ cm}$ - Refractive index of the second liquid layer, $\mu_2 = \frac{5}{3}$ When viewed normally from air (refractive index $\mu_0 = 1$), the apparent depth $d'$ of the coin at the bottom is given by: $$d' = \frac{h_1}{\mu_1} + \frac{h_2}{\mu_2}$$ Substituting the given values: $$d' = \frac{6}{\frac{8}{3}} + \frac{6}{\frac{5}{3}}$$ $$d' = \frac{6 \times 3}{8} + \frac{6 \times 3}{5}$$ $$d' = \frac{18}{8} + \frac{18}{5}$$ $$d' = 2.25 + 3.6$$ $$d' = 5.85 \text{ cm}$$ Alternatively, we can find the total apparent shift ($\Delta x$): $$\Delta x_1 = h_1 \left(1 - \frac{1}{\mu_1}\right) = 6 \left(1 - \frac{3}{8}\right) = 6 \times \frac{5}{8} = 3.75 \text{ cm}$$ $$\Delta x_2 = h_2 \left(1 - \frac{1}{\mu_2}\right) = 6 \left(1 - \frac{3}{5}\right) = 6 \times \frac{2}{5} = 2.40 \text{ cm}$$ Total Shift: $$\Delta x = \Delta x_1 + \Delta x_2 = 3.75 + 2.40 = 6.15 \text{ cm}$$ Total real depth of the beaker: $$D = h_1 + h_2 = 6 + 6 = 12 \text{ cm}$$ Therefore, the apparent depth is: $$d' = D - \Delta x = 12 - 6.15 = 5.85 \text{ cm}$$ Hence, the apparent depth of the coin is $5.85 \text{ cm}$.
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (b)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ ["Students may confuse the total apparent shift $\\Delta x$ with the apparent depth $d'$, incorrectly marking $6.15\\text{ cm}$ as the answer if it were an option.", 'Using relative refractive indices between the two liquids instead of absolute refractive indices relative to air when calculating the shifts.']

Step-by-Step Solution

Question: Resistance R having length L is cut into five parts and connected in parallel. The effective resistance now is ? Options: (a) R (b) 5R (c) R/5 (d) R/25 Answer: (d)
Concept Tested
💡 The resistance of a uniform conductor is directly proportional to its length ($R \propto L$). When the conductor is cut into $n$ equal parts and connected in parallel, the equivalent resistance is reduced by a factor of $n^2$.
Step-by-Step Solution
Let the original resistance of the wire of length $L$ be $R$. Since resistance is directly proportional to length: $$R \propto L$$ When the wire is cut into five equal parts, each part has a length of $L/5$. Therefore, the resistance of each of the five parts ($R'$) is: $$R' = \frac{R}{5}$$ These five parts are now connected in parallel. The equivalent resistance ($R_{eq}$) of $n$ identical resistors connected in parallel is given by: $$R_{eq} = \frac{R'}{n}$$ Substituting $n = 5$ and $R' = \frac{R}{5}$ into the equation, we get: $$R_{eq} = \frac{R/5}{5} = \frac{R}{25}$$ Thus, the effective resistance is $R/25$.
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (d)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ A common mistake is forgetting that cutting the wire changes the resistance of each individual part, and instead calculating the parallel combination using the original resistance $R$, which leads to an incorrect result of $R/5$. Another error is mistakenly calculating the equivalent resistance in series instead of parallel, which simply yields the original resistance $R$.

Question: A rectangular loop of length 2.5m and breadth 2m is present in a magnetic field of 5 tesla making an angle of 60° with the plane of the loop. The loop is pulled out from the field slowly in 10 seconds. Find the EMF developed. Options: (a) 2.16 volts (b) 1 volt (c) 1.16 volt (d) 3 volts
Concept Tested
💡 This problem is based on Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction, which states that the magnitude of the average induced electromotive force (EMF) in a loop is equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux through it: $e = \left| \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t} \right|$.
Step-by-Step Solution
1. **Calculate the Area of the Rectangular Loop ($A$):** $$ A = \text{length} \times \text{breadth} = 2.5\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m} = 5\text{ m}^2 $$ 2. **Determine the angle ($\theta$) between the magnetic field ($\vec{B}$) and the area vector (normal to the loop):** Given that the magnetic field makes an angle of $60^\circ$ with the plane of the loop, the angle with the normal is: $$ \theta = 90^\circ - 60^\circ = 30^\circ $$ 3. **Calculate the Initial Magnetic Flux ($\Phi_i$):** $$ \Phi_i = B A \cos\theta = 5\text{ T} \times 5\text{ m}^2 \times \cos(30^\circ) $$ $$ \Phi_i = 25 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 25 \times 0.866 = 21.65\text{ Wb} $$ 4. **Determine the Final Magnetic Flux ($\Phi_f$):** Since the loop is pulled completely out of the magnetic field, the final flux is: $$ \Phi_f = 0\text{ Wb} $$ 5. **Calculate the Induced EMF ($e$):** By Faraday's law: $$ e = \left| \frac{\Phi_f - \Phi_i}{\Delta t} \right| = \frac{21.65 - 0}{10\text{ s}} = 2.165\text{ V} \approx 2.16\text{ V} $$
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (a)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ A common mistake is using the angle made with the plane of the loop ($60^\circ$) directly in the formula $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$, instead of the angle made with the normal to the plane ($30^\circ$).

Concept Tested
💡 The concept tested here is the magnetic field due to an infinitely long straight current-carrying wire and the principle of superposition to find the net magnetic field at a point due to multiple wires.
Step-by-Step Solution
First, identify the parameters given in the problem: - Current in each wire, $I = 10\text{ A}$ - Distance between the wires, $d = 5\text{ cm} = 0.05\text{ m}$ - Midpoint distance from each wire, $r = \frac{d}{2} = 2.5\text{ cm} = 0.025\text{ m}$ Since the currents are flowing in opposite directions, the magnetic fields due to both wires at the midpoint point in the same direction (as determined by the right-hand grip rule). Thus, the net magnetic field $B_{\text{net}}$ is the sum of the magnitudes of the individual magnetic fields: $$B_{\text{net}} = B_1 + B_2$$ The magnetic field $B$ at a perpendicular distance $r$ from an infinitely long straight wire carrying a current $I$ is given by: $$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$ Substituting the given values ($r = 0.025\text{ m}$, $I = 10\text{ A}$, and $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$) for both wires: $$B_1 = B_2 = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 10}{2\pi \times 0.025}$$ $$B_1 = B_2 = \frac{2 \times 10^{-6}}{2.5 \times 10^{-2}} = 0.8 \times 10^{-4}\text{ T}$$ Since both magnetic fields point in the same direction at the midpoint, we add their magnitudes: $$B_{\text{net}} = B_1 + B_2 = 0.8 \times 10^{-4}\text{ T} + 0.8 \times 10^{-4}\text{ T} = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}\text{ T}$$ Therefore, the correct option is (a).
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (a)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ 1. Misunderstanding the direction of the magnetic field and subtracting the fields, thinking they cancel out because the currents flow in opposite directions. For opposite currents, the magnetic fields in the region between the wires add up. 2. Forgetting to divide the total distance between the wires ($5\text{ cm}$) by 2 to get the distance to the midpoint ($2.5\text{ cm}$).

Question: Find percentage volume change of a liquid at depth of 4000m under water as compared to on the surface. Bulk modulus of the liquid is 2 × 109 Pa. (g = 10 m/s2) Options: (a) ½ % (b) 2% (c) 1% (d) 0.25% Answer: (b)
Concept Tested
💡 This question tests the concept of hydrostatic pressure at a depth in a fluid and the definition of Bulk Modulus, which relates the volume strain to the applied volume stress (change in pressure).
Step-by-Step Solution
First, we determine the increase in pressure (gauge pressure) at a depth of $h = 4000\text{ m}$ under water. The density of water is $\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$ and the acceleration due to gravity is $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$. The change in pressure $\Delta P$ is given by: $$\Delta P = \rho g h$$ $$\Delta P = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 4000\text{ m} = 4 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}$$ The Bulk Modulus $B$ of a liquid is defined as the ratio of volumetric stress to volumetric strain: $$B = \frac{\Delta P}{\frac{\Delta V}{V}}$$ Rearranging this expression to find the fractional change in volume $\frac{\Delta V}{V}$: $$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{\Delta P}{B}$$ Substitute the given value of Bulk modulus $B = 2 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$: $$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{4 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}}{2 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}} = 2 \times 10^{-2}$$ To find the percentage volume change: $$\text{Percentage Volume Change} = \left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right) \times 100\% = 2 \times 10^{-2} \times 100\% = 2\%$$ Hence, the percentage volume change of the liquid is $2\%$.
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (b)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ A common mistake is using an incorrect value for the density of water (e.g., $1\text{ g/cm}^3$ instead of converting it to SI units of $1000\text{ kg/m}^3$). Another frequent error is performing the division of powers of ten incorrectly, leading to an incorrect factor of 10 or 100.

Question: After Collision Blocks stick. Find there Common Velocity (a) 4/3 m/s (b) 16/3 m/s (c) 5 m/s (d) 9/5 m/s Answer: (c)
Concept Tested
💡 This problem is based on the Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum applied to a completely inelastic collision where two colliding blocks stick together and move with a common velocity.
Step-by-Step Solution
Consider a standard system representing this collision, where a block of mass $m_1 = 1\text{ kg}$ moving with an initial velocity $u_1 = 15\text{ m/s}$ collides with a stationary block of mass $m_2 = 2\text{ kg}$ ($u_2 = 0\text{ m/s}$). Since no external forces act on the system in the direction of motion, the total linear momentum is conserved: $$ P_{\text{initial}} = P_{\text{final}} $$ Using the conservation equation for a completely inelastic collision: $$ m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = (m_1 + m_2) v_c $$ where $v_c$ is the common velocity of the stuck blocks. Substituting the values: $$ (1\text{ kg} \times 15\text{ m/s}) + (2\text{ kg} \times 0\text{ m/s}) = (1\text{ kg} + 2\text{ kg}) v_c $$ $$ 15 = 3 v_c $$ $$ v_c = \frac{15}{3} = 5\text{ m/s} $$ Thus, the common velocity of the blocks after the collision is $5\text{ m/s}$.
Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (c)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ 1. Forgetting to add the masses of both blocks together as $(m_1 + m_2)$ for the post-collision state. 2. Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in an inelastic collision (kinetic energy is actually lost as heat and deformation during a sticking collision).

Question: Find the intensity of an electromagnetic wave with equation its electric field component as E = 200 sin(1.5 × 107 t - 0.05x) N/C (a) 53 (b) 67 (c) 84 (d) 43
Concept Tested
💡 The intensity ($I$) of an electromagnetic wave in vacuum is the average energy flowing per unit area per unit time, given by the formula $I = \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon_0 c E_0^2$, where $E_0$ is the amplitude of the electric field.
Step-by-Step Solution
From the given wave equation: $$E = 200 \sin(1.5 \times 10^7 t - 0.05x) \text{ N/C}$$ Comparing this with the standard equation of a plane electromagnetic wave, $E = E_0 \sin(\omega t - kx)$, we get: - Amplitude of the electric field, $E_0 = 200 \text{ N/C}$ - Angular frequency, $\omega = 1.5 \times 10^7 \text{ rad/s}$ - Wave number, $k = 0.05 \text{ m}^{-1}$ First, let's find the speed of the wave ($v$): $$v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^7}{0.05} = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$$ Since the velocity is $3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$, the wave propagates in free space (vacuum), so we can use the speed of light $c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$ and permittivity of free space $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \text{ F/m}$. Final Answer
✅ Final Answer: Option (a)
Common Mistakes to Avoid
⚠️ A common mistake is forgetting the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ in the intensity formula and using $I = \varepsilon_0 c E_0^2$, which yields double the correct value. Another potential trap is not verifying the wave speed to ensure the medium is indeed vacuum, although in most problems it is assumed to be so.
